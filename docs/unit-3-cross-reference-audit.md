## Scope and method

Audited note revision: `54d66ac`. This report checks all four 48-line Unit 3 topic sheets and the 48-line supplement together: 240 note lines. File references below point to the plain-text sources under `website/public/notes/unit-3/`. The four topic sheets, their coloring, and the supplement have not been edited by this audit.

The review used Fiveable as a central breadth check, alongside OER Project's AP course and Freemanpedia's Unit 3 administration resources. The College Board framework controls the distinction between required developments and illustrative examples; historical references check dates and mechanisms. An example's absence from the CED's illustrative list does not make it educationally unimportant. Conversely, a name appearing in a review guide does not automatically make it a compulsory fact in every concise study sheet.

This is a concept/coverage cross-reference with targeted historical verification, not a claim that every sentence was independently established from archival primary documents. No conclusions about future question frequency are inferred.

### How to state the Abbas example accurately

- Chaldiran (1514) belongs to **Ismail I**, not Abbas I. Abbas ruled 1588–1629.
- Abbas expanded a ghulam counterweight; he did not eliminate all Qizilbash cavalry.
- Many recruits came from enslaved Caucasian Christian populations, converted to Islam, and entered military or administrative service. Do not describe this as exactly the Ottoman devshirme levy.
- Dependence on the shah for service and advancement reduced dependence on autonomous tribal chiefs. Paying standing forces made royal revenue important.
- [Iranica's Safavid army article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/army-iii/) describes ghulams in cavalry and high state office. Do not identify the whole ghulam corps with the separate musket infantry/artillery arms.
- Isfahan's royal architecture and crown/silk revenue would extend this example to culture and finance, but the counterweight-and-dependence mechanism is the first priority.

## Things the comparison does not justify flagging as errors or mandatory gaps

- **Jagirs:** supplement:15–16 already says revenue, not ownership, and **many**, not all, assignments were transferable. The wording accommodates hereditary/nontransferable exceptions. [Hansraj College's teaching text](https://www.hansrajcollege.ac.in/hCPanel/uploads/elearning/elearning_document/Mughal_Jagirdari_and_Mansabdari_System.pdf) explicitly describes those exceptions. No factual correction is established merely because the exceptions are not all named.
- **Qing military and civil institutions:** hereditary banners and their Manchu/Mongol/Han membership are already at 3.4:9–10; retained Confucian exams and elite cooperation are at 3.2:6–7 and 3.4:20–23. Linking them could improve presentation, but the institutions themselves are not absent. Do not invent an absolute “all military offices Manchu, all civil offices Han” split.
- **Reformation and Sikhism:** the Protestant/Catholic changes, printing, princes, Trent, Jesuits, confessional violence, older Sunni–Shi'a division, distinct Sikh identity, Guru Nanak, equality, and langar are already present. The narrower Calvinist definition gap does not mean the entire belief topic is missing.
- **Mughal policy variation:** Akbar's accommodation and Aurangzeb's jizya restoration are already in 3.3:43–45 and 3.4:45. They should not be rediscovered as a new omission by reading the supplement alone.
- **Additional optional cases:** Henry VIII/Anglican royal supremacy, French intendants, Peter I's administrative/church reforms, and a named religious-war settlement could broaden evidence. They are not automatic must-adds solely because a course resource includes them. Scientific Revolution or maritime empire detail should not crowd out the actual Unit 3 learning objectives.
- **Cumulative American foundations:** Unit 1.4 already provides mit'a, quipu, Inti/ancestral legitimation, tribute, and sacrifice. Unit 3 needs to apply that evidence to its own period and explanations, not unnecessarily reteach all of it.

## Course materials as a breadth check

Fiveable is particularly useful here because its Abbas entry connects military personnel, tribal power, finance, and monuments rather than simply naming the shah. OER Project and Freemanpedia expose other state-specific examples and guiding questions. The source comparison therefore goes beyond checking only the CED's short illustrative list.

At the same time, cross-reference at the level of the claim: a course page may simplify a date, classify a table entry badly, or mix later context into a unit. This audit does not replace a correct statement in the notes merely because another review page phrases it differently. It does not copy “all empires used gunpowder,” conflate devshirme with a tax system, or reduce Sikhism to a mixture without its own identity.

## Conclusion and next-edit priorities

The current set covers the four learning objectives thematically, but **it is not a comprehensive inventory of the substantive course material found across these resources**. Abbas is not the only meaningful omission.

The first content priorities are Safavid centralization, Ottoman North Africa/Egypt, the Morocco–Songhai example, and the predestination definition. The next strongest additions are Ottoman caliphal claims, Qing Buddhist patronage, and a Russian authority example, followed by a brief application of already-learned Mexica ritual evidence.

These priorities do not imply that every source's extra name must be memorized. Nor does this audit establish that every remaining omission can be repaired within 48 supplemental lines without sacrificing useful content. That trade-off needs an explicit edit plan, not another blanket assurance of completeness. No study-note edits, commit, push, or deployment were performed in this research pass.
