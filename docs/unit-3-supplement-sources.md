## Millet arrangements and state oversight

- Efrat Aviv, [“Millet System in the Ottoman Empire,” Oxford Bibliographies](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62361/reference-article/554566607), introduction: communal authority involved “limited power to regulate their own affairs,” under Ottoman supremacy. The supplement therefore distinguishes internal communal affairs from independent sovereignty or religious equality. Non-Muslim access to Islamic courts also makes communal legal jurisdiction nonexclusive.
- [“Millet in Ottoman Documents,” Nationalities Papers / Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nationalities-papers/article/millet-in-ottoman-documents-religious-confessional-or-ethnicnational-collectives-of-nonmuslims/0D85ED642595B3726C1883E37A613220), cautions against projecting a uniform nineteenth-century model backward. The supplement uses *millet* as a religious grouping and explicitly describes evolving, nonuniform practices in the early modern period.

## Jizya, status, and exemptions

- [UCLA MANAS, “Aurangzeb’s Fatwa on Jizya”](https://southasia.ucla.edu/history-politics/mughals-and-medieval/aurangzeb/aurangzebs-fatwa-jizya/), translated by Anver Emon from *Al-Fatawa al-Alamgiriyyah*, provides a period legal text. It distinguishes negotiated and imposed arrangements, rates linked to resources, and exemptions for women, children, and specified people unable to work or pay. This is a Mughal legal source, not a claim that every Ottoman or Mughal locality implemented identical rules.
- Jizya is a poll-tax obligation within a wider legal and political arrangement. It did not itself create a right to communal self-government. Protected status, fiscal obligations, religious institutions, and the ruler’s supremacy are related but distinct mechanisms.
- Akbar’s abolition in 1564 and Aurangzeb’s restoration in 1679 are included as a Mughal comparison, not as dates in the history of Ottoman communal institutions.

## Reading the qualifications

Words such as “eligible,” “could,” and “in theory” are deliberate. The notes distinguish tax liability from universal collection, communal authority from exclusive jurisdiction, and claims of legitimate rule from unrestricted practical control. They do not claim that an empire’s treatment of religious communities stayed uniform across rulers, regions, or the whole 1450–1750 period.
