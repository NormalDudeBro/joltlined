# Unit 3 supplement: source notes

The two-page supplement adds 48 lines without changing any existing topic sheet. Its short keyword highlights follow the existing Units 1–2 style. These source notes are outside the printable note text.

## Scope

The [College Board AP World History: Modern course framework](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-world-history-modern-course-and-exam-description.pdf), Unit 3 topic pages 69–72 (PDF pages 76–79), supplies the learning objectives and examples. The supplement prioritizes the definitions and mechanisms needed to explain communal government, fiscal dependence, sacred authority, religious reform, sectarian leadership, and elite cooperation. It is additional review, not an invented official topic 3.5.

## Combined-unit gap audit

The audit read all four main topic sheets plus the original supplement together. Reformation, Sunni–Shi'a rivalry, Sikhism, gunpowder, bureaucratic recruitment, taxation, Versailles, Mughal monuments, and Qing portraits are already present in the main sheets. Topic coverage alone, however, did not establish explanatory sufficiency. The revision now asks whether a reader has the definition, operating mechanism, and consequence needed for a supported explanation, rather than merely a named institution or another optional example.

| Learning objective | Existing coverage | Supplemental priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1: explain expansion | Weapons, finance, alliances, trade, six regional empires, rivalries, and limits | Existing military and logistical mechanisms remain in the main sheets; avoid displacing missing definitions with extra battle names. |
| 3.2: explain consolidation and legitimacy | Devshirme, Janissaries, Qing exams, zamindars, tax farming, religious claims, architecture, and local elites | Explain delegated administration, advance tax-farming payments, transferable jagirs, peasants' need for silver, divine-right obedience, and the conditional Mandate of Heaven. |
| 3.3: explain belief continuity and change | Protestant and Catholic reform, printing, confessional politics, the older Sunni–Shi'a split, Sikhism, and varying Mughal policies | Define indulgences, justification, Trent, Sunni succession, and Twelver religious authority; connect sales, princely interests, and state patronage to change. |
| 3.4: compare methods of increasing influence | Named military, recruitment, fiscal, religious, architectural, and diversity comparisons | Retain labor-versus-cash extraction and court supervision; explain Rajput identity, the Hindu-majority context, financial incentives in court history, and alternate attendance. |

The CED's Inca temple, Mexica sacrifice, and Songhai Islam cases are **illustrative examples**, not a requirement to memorize every listed example. Inca labor and sacred ancestry plus Songhai Islam remain as geographic variety; additional optional illustrations were not allowed to crowd out missing explanatory information. This is not a prediction of which examples a future exam will use.

To keep exactly 48 supplemental lines, lower-priority additional cases were replaced by the missing definitions and causal links. The essential jizya-versus-autonomy distinction and the limitations on community authority remain. The four main topic sheets are unchanged. The supplement remains a selective review, not a claim that every optional fact or every possible stimulus is reproduced.

## Public AP question examples

- [2024 Set 1 scoring guidelines](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap24-sg-world-history-modern-set-1.pdf), Short Answer Question 1(c), asks why Mughal rulers encouraged Hindu–Muslim interactions. Accepted explanations include reducing rebellion, securing acceptance of Mughal rule, and drawing on the population's economic, political, and military contributions. The revised supplement therefore links Rajput cooperation and cultural patronage to governing capacity instead of merely labeling a ruler tolerant.
- [2024 Set 2 scoring guidelines](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap24-sg-world-history-modern-set-2.pdf), Short Answer Question 1(c), asks how history writing and art could legitimize state power in 1200–1750. The supplement adds a named court-history example and attention to the source's purpose, alongside the architecture already in 3.2 and 3.4.
- These are public examples of assessable reasoning, **not evidence of question frequency or predictions of the next exam**. Specific evidence must still answer the actual prompt and explain how or why it supports a claim.

## Definitions and mechanisms: verification

- The [Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 1471–1473](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/x_indulgences.html), distinguishes remission of temporal punishment from forgiveness of sin's guilt. The note therefore does not teach that Catholic doctrine sold forgiveness or guaranteed salvation. This official modern definition clarifies the doctrinal distinction, not the uniformity of sixteenth-century sales practices.
- [Hansraj College, University of Delhi, *The Mughal Jagirdari and Mansabdari System*](https://www.hansrajcollege.ac.in/hCPanel/uploads/elearning/elearning_document/Mughal_Jagirdari_and_Mansabdari_System.pdf), describes revenue assignments, cash-salary equivalents, and transfers that checked nobles' local roots. It also describes hereditary, nontransferable *watan jagirs*, so the note deliberately says **many**, not all, jagirs were transferable.
- Britannica's editorial articles on [Sunni Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sunni), [Shi'a Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Shii), and [Twelver Shi'a](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Twelver-Shia) distinguish recognition of the first four caliphs from authority through Ali's line and the Twelver lineage of Imams. These earlier developments are necessary context for the Ottoman–Safavid labels, not events newly originating in 1450–1750. The Safavid shahs are not presented as themselves being the twelve Imams.
- Tokugawa lordship, samurai stipends, and alternate attendance are distinct mechanisms. Regular court residence increased supervision; it was not identical in its legal obligations to French court attendance, and it did not itself pay samurai.

## Additional evidence and chronology

- The [V&A's Akbarnama collection record](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O9441/akbar-painting-kesav-kalan/) identifies Akbar as the commissioner, Abu'l Fazl as the court historian, and the illustrated manuscript as an official chronicle of his reign. Its depiction of imperial victory supports discussing court-sponsored representations and legitimacy, not assuming that a flattering image proves effective or just government.
- Rajputs are identified as Hindu warrior elites in Akbar's service; their troops and support are connected to rule over a Hindu-majority population. The public 2024 Set 1 Question 1(b) painter example was reviewed, but the supplement now gives priority to the governing mechanism rather than adding another artistic case.
- Inca mit'a and sun temples refer to that state during the pre-conquest part of 1450–1750. They do not imply independent Inca imperial rule lasted through 1750. The labor comparison concerns precolonial state obligations, not the later Spanish colonial mining draft.
- The note that both reformations spread Christianity follows the explicit Unit 3 development KC-4.1.VI.i; it does not shift this sheet into a general survey of maritime missions or later Protestant overseas expansion.

## Millet arrangements and state oversight

- Efrat Aviv, [“Millet System in the Ottoman Empire,” Oxford Bibliographies](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62361/reference-article/554566607), introduction: communal authority involved “limited power to regulate their own affairs,” under Ottoman supremacy. The supplement therefore distinguishes internal communal affairs from independent sovereignty or religious equality. Non-Muslim access to Islamic courts also makes communal legal jurisdiction nonexclusive.
- [“Millet in Ottoman Documents,” Nationalities Papers / Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nationalities-papers/article/millet-in-ottoman-documents-religious-confessional-or-ethnicnational-collectives-of-nonmuslims/0D85ED642595B3726C1883E37A613220), cautions against projecting a uniform nineteenth-century model backward. The short note uses *millet* for limited communal religious governance; it does not assert that identical, fully formalized institutions existed throughout the period.

## Jizya, status, and exemptions

- [UCLA MANAS, “Aurangzeb’s Fatwa on Jizya”](https://southasia.ucla.edu/history-politics/mughals-and-medieval/aurangzeb/aurangzebs-fatwa-jizya/), translated by Anver Emon from *Al-Fatawa al-Alamgiriyyah*, provides a period legal text. It distinguishes negotiated and imposed arrangements, rates linked to resources, and exemptions for women, children, and specified people unable to work or pay. This is a Mughal legal source, not a claim that every Ottoman or Mughal locality implemented identical rules.
- Jizya is a poll-tax obligation within a wider legal and political arrangement. It did not itself create a right to communal self-government. Protected status, fiscal obligations, religious institutions, and the ruler’s supremacy are related but distinct mechanisms.
- Akbar–Aurangzeb policy variation remains in the main 3.3 sheet. The revised supplement uses its limited space for additional evidence rather than repeating those dates.

## Reading the qualifications

Words such as “eligible,” “could,” and “in theory” are deliberate. The notes distinguish tax liability from universal collection, communal authority from exclusive jurisdiction, and claims of legitimate rule from unrestricted practical control. They do not claim that an empire’s treatment of religious communities stayed uniform across rulers, regions, or the whole 1450–1750 period.
